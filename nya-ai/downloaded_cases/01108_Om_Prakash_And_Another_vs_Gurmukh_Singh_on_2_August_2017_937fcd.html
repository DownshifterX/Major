Uttarakhand High Court

Om Prakash And Another vs Gurmukh Singh on 2 August, 2017

Author: Lok Pal Singh

Bench: Lok Pal Singh

                                  1




IN THE HIGH COURT OF UTTARAKHAND AT NAINITAL

                    Second Appeal No.74 of 2008

Om Prakash and another                         .......Appellants
                                Vs.
Gurmukh Singh                                  .......Respondent

Mr. Atul Kumar Bansal, Advocate for the appellants.
Mr. Gopal K. Verma, Advocate for the respondent.

Hon'ble Lok Pal Singh, J.

Present appeal is directed against the judgment and decree dated 09.07.2008 passed by the first appellate court in Civil Appeal No. 2 of 2005 Om Prakash vs. Gurmukh Singh whereby the learned first appellate court has affirmed the judgment and decree dated 30.11.2004 passed by the Civil Judge (Jr. Div.) Kashipur, District Udham Singh Nagar in Original Suit No. 90 of 2001.

2. Brief facts, of the case, are that plaintiff/respondent filed suit for prohibitory injunction being O.S. No. 90 of 2001 Gurmukh Singh vs. Om Prakash and another with the averments that plaintiff, his brother and nephew Anshu Kumar are the recorded owner of the khasra nos. 268, 481, 501 and 503 (total 1.036 hect.) situated at Village Barkheda Pandey, Tehsil Kashipur, District Udham Singh Nagar and the suit for partition under section 176 of U.P.Z.A.L.R. was filed by the plaintiff bearing revenue suit no. 22 of 2007 Gurmukh Singh vs. Amar Singh and others. The suit was decreed on 03.07.2001 and final decree was prepared on 16.04.2001 and land in dispute came into the share of plaintiff/respondent. It is contended that plaintiff is the exclusive owner and in possession of the land in question and the defendants/appellants who have no concern with the land in question are threatening the plaintiff to dispossess him from the land in question.

2

3. The defendants/appellants contested the suit and filed their written statements and admitted the averments of paragraph no. 1 of the plaint that the plaintiff becomes the owner of the property in dispute in view of partition decree. The defendants have contended that the plaintiff/respondent sold 1/3 share of Khasra no. 503 to one Shri Sohran Singh and remaining land was mortgaged to the defendants on 15.11.1999. In lieu thereof, plaintiff was paid Rs. 26,000/- by defendant no.

1. It is further contended that mortgaged deed (Rahanama) was executed and it was notarized by the Notary and further contended that the suit has been filed on false ground and is barred by provision of Section 34 of the Specific Relief Act.

4. On the pleadings of the parties, learned trial court framed the following issues:-

(i) Whether the plaintiff is the owner and in possession of the land in dispute?
(ii) Whether the plaintiff has any cause of action?
(iii) Whether the suit is barred by section 34 of Specific Relief Act?
(iv) To what relief plaintiff is entitled for?

5. On behalf of the plaintiff Gurmukh Singh examined himself as P.W. 1 and Kallu Singh was examined as P.W.2. The documentary evidence was filed on behalf of the plaintiff/respondent which includes copy of khatoni 9C, copy of khasra no. 10C, copy of final decree prepared in favour of the plaintiff whereby the land in question came into the possession of the plaintiff by way of partition.

3

6. On behalf of the defendant/appellant, alongwith list 17C copy of alleged Rehnama, notice 19C, copy of postal receipt and A.D. reply notice were filed. Defendant Om Prakash got examined himself as D.W.1 and Sompal Singh was examined as D.W. 2.

7. The learned trial court while recording its findings on issue no. 1 has caterogically recorded that name of plaintiff/respondent is recorded in Khasra and Khatoni as Bhumidhar, which is evident from the copy of khasra and khatoni and the property in dispute came into the possession of plaintiff in view of partition decree, paper no. 11/2 is the final decree. The learned trial court further recorded its finding that alleged Rehnama paper no. 18C/3 seems to be executed by Gurmukh Das while the plaintiff is Gurmukh Singh. Since document 18C/3 alleged Rehnama is not a registered document, therefore this document is inadmissible in the evidence. Considering the provision of section 39 of Transfer of Property Act and provision of section 49 of Indian Registration Act, the learned trial court arrived to the conclusion that the document paper no. 18C/3 has not been proved even otherwise it is not admissible in evidence. After considering the evidence of both the parties, the trial court recorded categorical findings of possession that the plaintiff/respondent is the owner and in possession of the property in dispute and decided issue no. 1 in favour of the plaintiff/respondent.

8. The trial court, after perusal of the plaint allegation arrived to the conclusion that on 21.10.2001, defendant tried to take possession over the plaintiff's property and tried to take possession over it, therefore, plaintiff/ 4 respondent has the cause of action to institute the suit and decided issue no. 2 positively in favour of the plaintiff/respondent.

9. Issue no. 3 was framed by the trial court "whether the suit is barred by section 34 of the Specific Relief Act"? The learned trial court has categorically recorded its finding that the plaintiff/respondent is in possession over the property in dispute and alleged Rehnama has not been proved by the defendant. The learned trial court placed reliance on the judgment of Dharam Singh vs. Bhopal Singh 1990 (90) page 37 and has held that the suit is not barred by section 34 of Specific Relief Act when the possession of the plaintiff is proved over the land in question. The trial court has recorded its findings that the plaintiff/respondent is in possession over the land in dispute therefore the suit is not barred by section 34 of Specific Relief Act and decided issue no. 3 in favour of the plaintiff/ respondent.

10. The trial court recorded its findings on issue nos. 1 to 3 in favour of the plaintiff/respondent, therefore the learned trial court also decided issue no. 4 in favour of the plaintiff/respondent and decreed the suit of prohibitory injunction by judgment and decree dated 30.11.2004. feeling aggrieved by the judgment and decree passed by the trial court, civil appeal no. 2 of 2005 Om Prakash vs. Gurmukh Singh was filed by the appellant.

10. The first appellate court recorded its categorical findings on all the issues framed by the trial court. The first appellate court has also recorded its finding that the alleged Rehanama paper no.18C was having the value of 5 more than ` 100/- which requires to be registered. Neither the alleged Rehnama was registered, nor proved by the defendants / appellants.

11. In view of section 17 of Registration Act, the documents having value of more than ` 100, requires its registration. The defendants/appellants did not took the plea for taking benefit of section 53 (A) of Transfer of Property Act. The defendants/appellants took plea before the first appellate court that they are entitled to get benefit of section 53(A) of Transfer of Property Act. Since, the ground was raised by the defendant/appellant before the first appellate court; the first appellate court also recorded its finding on point of benefit of section 53(A) of the Transfer of Property Act. The first appellate court by placing reliance upon the judgment of Moolchand Bakhroo vs. Rohan and others reported in 2002(1) 246SC, recorded its finding that Section 53(A) of Transfer of Property Act is neither pleaded by the defendants/appellants nor any evidence to this effect has been read by the defendants/appellants. Therefore, defendants/appellants are not entitled for the benefit of Section of 53(A) of Transfer of Property Act. The first appellate court after recording its finding on all the issues so framed by the trial court pleased to dismiss the appeal filed by the defendant/appellant. Hence, this second appeal.

12. This second appeal was admitted on the following substantial question of law:-

(i) Whether the alleged transfer with possession by the plaintiff to defendant, as pleaded in the written statement, amount to a 6 deemed sale of the Bhumidhari land as provided under section 164 fo U.P.Z.A & L.R. Act?

(ii) Whether the defendant/appellants are entitled to take benefit of provisions of section 53A of Transfer of Property Act?

(iii) Whether the alleged mortgaged deed (Rehanama) dated 15.11.1999 is inadmissible for want of registration?

13. The substantial questions of law framed by this Court have been framed on the basis of the substantial question of law framed by the defendant/appellant in the memo of appeal. As the substantial questions of law were framed by the defendant/appellant in the memo of the appeal, this Court without examining pleading as well as without being satisfied itself as to whether substantial question of law framed in the memo of the appeal are going to the root of the case framed substantial questions of law. Since, substantial questions of law were framed, therefore same are being decided.

14. Answer to substantial question of law no. (i) From the perusal of the written statement the defendants/appellants have not averred in any of the paragraph that the possession was transferred to the defendants in view of section 164 of U.P.Z.A. and L.R. Act. Since, neither there is any pleading nor any evidence lead by the defendant/appellant in regard to the contents of section 164 of the Act, therefore, in my view substantial question of law was framed by this Court without going into the pleadings of the defendant/ 7 appellant. It is significant to mention here that the defendants/ appellants did not took the plea that they were put in possession by the plaintiff/respondent in lieu of mortgaged deed dated 15.11.1999 and in view of provision of section 164 of U.P.Z.A. & L.R. Act, they mature their rights on the basis of the alleged possession. Neither the benefit of section 53A of Transfer of Property Act nor the benefit of section 164 of U.P.Z.A &L.R. Act was claimed in the written statement. First time in the memo of the appeal ground was taken that the appellants are entitled to get the benefit of Section 53A of Transfer of Property Act. In absence of pleadings, nothing can be looked into it.

15. Answer to substantial question of law no. (ii):-

The substantial question of law no. 2 has been framed to the effect whether the defendant/appellant is entitled for the protection of section 53A of Transfer of Property Act. The defendants/appellants have not fulfilled the requirement of section 53A of the Transfer of the Property Act. Neither they have pleaded in their case nor have they adduced any evidence for getting benefit of section 53A of the Act.
Their Lordships of Hon'ble Apex Court in the case of Bondar Singh and others vs. Nihal Singh and other reported in (2003) 4SCC 161 has held that no evidence can be looked into the pleadings. The relevant paragraphs are reproduced below:-
6. It appears that having failed to obtain possession of the suit land through lawful means, the defendants tried to dispossess the plaintiffs forcibly which led to the present suit being filed on 15.4.1972. The claim of the defendants regarding taking possession of suit land from plaintiffs in 1957-58 having been found to be false, it follows that the defendants never 8 came into possession of the suit land. Another significant conclusion which follows from these facts is that the defendants started asserting their title to the suit land since at least 1956 when they issued the notice Exhibit P.6 while the plaintiffs have been denying their title to the suit land and were setting up their own title to the same. This lends support to the plea of adverse possession set up by the plaintiffs. It will be seen from this clear and clinching evidence on record that the plaintiffs were in continuous and uninterrupted possession of the suit land since 1931 and they had been setting up a hostile title thereto as against the defendants. The defendants were asserting their title to the land since 1956. They had however failed to get possession of the suit land. The plea of adverse possession raised by the plaintiff is thus clearly established.
7. As regards the plea of sub tenancy (shikmi) argued on behalf of the defendants by their learned counsel, first we may note that this plea was never taken in the written statement the way it has been put forth now. The written statement is totally vague and lacking in material particulars on this aspect. There is nothing to support this plea except some alleged revenue entries. It is settled law that in the absence of a plea no amount of evidence led in relation thereto can be looked into. Therefore, in the absence of a clear plea regarding sub tenancy (shikmi) the defendants cannot be allowed to build up a case of sub tenancy (shikmi). Had the defendants taken such a plea it would have found place as an issue in the suit. We have perused the issues framed in the suit. There is no issue on the point.

It is not pleaded by the defendants/ appellants that they are entitled to get benefit of section 53A of Transfer of Property Act and no evidence has been led by the defendants/appellants in this regard. The benefit of section 53A of Transfer of Property Act is not available to the defendants/ appellants, as the defendants have to prove certain facts to get the benefit of Section 53A of the Transfer of Property Act. Section 53A of Transfer of Property Act is reproduced below:-

[53A. Part performance.--Where any person contracts to transfer for consideration any immoveable property by writing signed by him or on his behalf from which the terms necessary to constitute the transfer can be ascertained with reasonable certainty, and the transferee has, in part performance of the contract, taken possession of the property or any part thereof, or the transferee, being already in possession, continues in possession in part performance of the contract and has done some act in furtherance of the contract, and the transferee has performed or is willing to perform his part of the contract, 9 then, notwithstanding that the contract, though required to be registered, has not been registered, or, where there is an instrument of transfer, that the transfer has not been completed in the manner prescribed therefor by the law for the time being in force, the transferor or any person claiming under him shall be debarred from enforcing against the transferee and persons claiming under him any right in respect of the property of which the transferee has taken or continued in possession, other than a right expressly provided by the terms of the contract:
Provided that nothing in this section shall affect the rights of a transferee for consideration who has no notice of the contract or of the part performance thereof.] The plea of Section 53A was taken by the defendant/ appellant before the first appellate court. From the perusal of the contents of the written statement it would reveal that this plea has never been taken by the defendants/appellants in its pleadings before the trial court that he is entitled to the benefit of section 53A of Transfer of property Act. Neither the plea of Section 53A of Transfer of Property Act was taken nor any evidence was led by the defence in this regard. It is settled preposition of the law that evidence cannot be looked in absence of any pleadings. Since, defendants/appellants did not raise plea of taking benefit of section 53A of the Transfer of the Property Act in their written statements, therefore they are not entitled to get benefit of section 53A of the Transfer of the Property Act. I am of the considered opinion that substantial question of law no.
(ii) is also not arises in this appeal. Same is decided against defendants/ appellants.

16. Answer to substantial question of law no. (iii):-

Mortgaged deed (Rehanama) paper no. 18C has been filed by the plaintiff/respondent allegedly executed by Gurmukh Das in favour of the defendants/appellants 10 Omprakash. Value of transaction @ ` 26000/- has been mentioned in the Rehanama paper no. 18C. In rehanama it has been mentioned that amount was paid to the plaintiff in the presence of the witnesses. For that purpose statement of Sompal Singh is relevant. In his cross-examination he made a statement that in his presence no amount was given at the time of execution of the document. Since, an amount of ` 26,000/- has been mentioned as transaction amount in mortgaged deed (unregistered), in view of section 17 of the Registration Act document having value of ` 100 requires compulsory registration. Section 17 of the Registration Act is reproduced below:-

17. In view of section 49 of the Registration Act, document which requires registration, is not admissible in evidence. Section 49 of the Registration Act is reproduced below:-

49. Effect of non-registration of documents required to be registered.--No document required by section 17 59[or by any provision of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 (4 of 1882)], 60[or of any other law for the time being in force] to be registered shall--
(a) affect any immovable property comprised therein, or 61[(b) confer any power or create any right or relationship, or
(c) be received as evidence of any transaction affecting such property or conferring such power 62 [or creating such right or relationship], unless it has been registered:
63[Provided that an unregistered document affecting immovable property and required by this Act or the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 (4 of 1882), to be registered may be received 64[* * *] as evidence of part performance of a contract for the purpose of Section 53-A of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 (4 of 1882), or as evidence of any collateral transaction not required to be effected by registered instrument.]

18. Though, substantial question of law has not been defined under section 100 of Code of Civil Procedure but the Hon'ble Apex Court in the case of Santosh Hazari vs. Purushottam Tiwari reported in 2001 (3) 179 has held that the substantial question of law means of having substance, essential, real, of sound worth, important and considerable. The relevant paragraphs are reproduced below:-

"12. The phrase "substantial question of law", as occurring in the amended Section 100 is not defined in the Code. The word substantial, as qualifying "question of law", means - of having substance, essential, real, of sound worth, important or considerable. It is to be understood as something in contradistinction with - technical, of no substance or consequence, or academic merely. However, it is clear that the Legislature has chosen not to qualify the scope of substantial question of law by suffixing the words of general importance as has been done in many other provisions such as Section 109 of the Code or Article 133(1)(a) of the Constitution. The substantial question of law on which a second appeal shall be 13 heard need not necessarily be a substantial question of law of general importance. In Guran Ditta Vs. T. Ram Ditta, the phrase "substantial question of law" as it was employed in the last clause of the then existing Section 110 of the C.P.C. (since omitted by the Amendment Act, 1973) came up for consideration and their Lordships held that it did not mean a substantial question of general importance but a substantial question of law which was involved in the case as between the parties. In Sir Chunilal V. Mehta & Sons Ltd. Vs. Century Spg. & Mfg. Co. Ltd. the Constitution Bench expressed agreement with the following view taken by a Full Bench of Madras High Court in Rimmalapudi Subba Rao Vs. Noony Veeraju:
"[W]hen a question of law is fairly arguable, where there is room for difference of opinion on it or where the Court thought it necessary to deal with that question at some length and discuss alternative view, then the question would be a substantial question of law. On the other hand if the question was practically covered by the decision of the highest Court or if the general principles to be applied in determining the question are well settled and the only question was of applying those principles to the particular fact of the case it would not be a substantial question of law."

and laid down the following test as proper test, for determining whether a question of law raised in the case is substantial:-

"The proper test for determining whether a question of law raised in the case is substantial would, in our opinion, be whether it is of general public importance or whether it directly and substantially affects the rights of the parties and if so whether it is either an open question in the sense that it is not finally settled by this Court or by the Privy Council or by the Federal Court or is not free from difficulty or calls for discussion of alternative views. If the question is settled by the highest Court or the general principles to be applied in determining the question are well settled and there is a mere question of applying those principles or that the plea raised is palpably absurd the question would not be a substantial question of law."

13. In Deputy Commr., Hardoi, Vs. Rama Krishna Narain, also it was held that a question of law of importance to the parties was a substantial question of law entitling the appellant to certificate under (the then) Section 110 of the Code.

14. A point of law which admits of no two opinions may be a proposition of law but cannot be a 14 substantial question of law. To be substantial, a question of law must be debatable, not previously settled by law of the land or a binding precedent, and must have a material bearing on the decision of the case, if answered either way, in so far as the rights of the parties before it are concerned. To be a question of law involving in the case there must be first a foundation for it laid in the pleadings and the question should emerge from the sustainable findings of fact arrived at by court of facts and it must be necessary to decide that question of law for a just and proper decision of the case. An entirely new point raised for the first time before the High Court is not a question involved in the case unless it goes to the root of the matter. It will, therefore, depend on the facts and circumstance of each case whether a question of law is a substantial one and involved in the case, or not; the paramount overall consideration being the need for striking a judicious balance between the indispensable obligation to do justice at all stages and impelling necessity of avoiding prolongation in the life of any lis.

19. Their Lordships of Hon'ble Apex Court in the case of Damodar Lal vs. Sohan Devi and others reported in (2016) 3SCC 78 held as follows:

"9. In Krishan v. Backiam, it has been held at para 11 that:
"11. It may be mentioned that the first appellate court under section 96 CPC is the last court of facts. The High Court in second appeal under section 100 CPC cannot interfere with the findings of fact recorded by the first appellate court under Section 96 CPC. No doubt the findings of fact of the first appellate court can be challenged in second appeal on the ground that the said findings are based on no evidence or are perverse, but even in that case a question of law has to be formulated and framed by the High Court to that effect."

20. Alleged mortgaged deed (Rahanama) paper no.18C is unregistered document having value of more than ` 100/- therefore, mortgaged deed (Rahanama) paper no. 18C is not admissible in evidence.

21. Their Lordships of Hon'ble Apex Court in the case of K.B. Sah and Sons Private Limited vs. Development 15 Consultant Limited reported in (2008) 8 SCC 564 held as follows:-

29. As we have already noted that under the proviso to Section 49 of the Registration Act, an unregistered document can also be admitted into evidence for a collateral fact/collateral purpose, let us now look at the meaning of "collateral purpose" and then ascertain whether Clause 9 of the lease agreement can be looked into for such collateral purpose.
30. In Haran Chandra Chakrvarti Vs. Kaliprasanna Sarkar, it was held that the terms of a compulsorily registrable instrument are nothing less than a transaction affecting the property comprised in it. It was also held that to use such an instrument for the purpose of proving such a term would not be using it for a collateral purpose and that the question as to who is the tenant and on what terms he has been created a tenant are not collateral facts but they are important terms of the contract of tenancy, which cannot be proved by admission of an unregistered lease-deed into evidence.
31. The High Court in the impugned Judgment relied on a decision of the Allahabad High Court in the case of Ratan Lal Vs. Hari Shanker to hold that since the appellant wanted to extinguish the right of the respondent with the help of the unregistered tenancy, the same was not a collateral purpose. In Ratan lal's case, while discussing the meaning of the term "Collateral Purpose", the High Court had observed as follows :- (AIR pp. 180-81, para 4) "4. The second contention was that the partition deed, even if it was not registered could certainly be looked into for a collateral purpose....but the collateral purpose has a limited scope and meaning. It cannot be used for the purpose of saying that the deed created or declared or assigned or limited or extinguish the right to immovable property....term 'collateral purpose' would not permit the party to establish any of these acts from the deed."
32. In Bajaj Auto Limited vs. Behari Lal Kohli this Court observed that if a document is inadmissible for non-registration, all its terms are inadmissible including the one dealing with landlord's permission to his tenant to sub-let. It was also held in that decision that if a decree purporting to create a lease is inadmissible in evidence for want of registration, none of the terms of the lease can be admitted in evidence and that to use a document for the purpose of proving an important clause in the lease is not using it as a collateral purpose. Again this court in Rai Chand Jain Vs. Chandra Kanta Khosla reiterated the above and 16 observed in paragraph 10 as under /SCC p. 429, para
10) "10. ...the lease deed Ext. P-11 dated 19-

5-1978 executed both by the appellant and the respondent i.e. the landlady and the tenant, Rai Chand Jain, though unregistered can be considered for collateral purposes and as such the findings of the Appellate Authority to the effect that the said deed cannot be used for collateral purposes namely to show that the purpose was to lease out the demised premises for residential purposes of the tenant only is not at all legally correct. It is well settled that unregistered lease executed by both the parties can be looked into for collateral purposes. In the instant case the purpose of the lease is evident from the deed itself which is as follows: "The lessor hereby demises House No. 382, Sector 30- A, Chandigarh, to lessee for residential purposes only". This clearly evinces that the property in question was let out to the tenant for his residence only...."

20. In Rana Vidya Bhushan Singh Vs. Ratiram the following has been laid down:

"A document required by law to be registered, if unregistered, is inadmissible as evidence of a transaction affecting immovable property, but it may be admitted as evidence of collateral facts, or for any collateral purpose, that is for any purpose other than that of creating, declaring, assigning, limiting or extinguishing a right to immovable property. As stated by Mulla in his Indian Registration Act, 7th Edn., at p. 189 :
"The High Courts of Calcutta, Bombay, Allahabad, Madras, Patna, Lahore, Assam, Nagpur, Pepsu, Rajasthan, Orissa, Rangoon and Jammu & Kashmir; the former Chief Court of Oudh; the Judicial Commissioner's Court of Peshawar, Ajmer and Himachal Pradesh and the Supreme Court have held that a document which requires registration under Section 17 and which is not admissible for want of registration to prove a gift or mortgage or sale or lease is nevertheless admissible to prove the character of the possession of the person who holds under it."

34. From the principles laid down in the various decisions of this Court and the High Courts, as referred to hereinabove, it is evident that :-

1. A document required to be registered is not admissible into evidence under Section 49 of the Registration Act.
17
2. Such unregistered document can however be used as an evidence of collateral purpose as provided in the Proviso to Section 49 of the Registration Act.
3. A collateral transaction must be independent of, or divisible from, the transaction to effect which the law required registration.
4. A collateral transaction must be a transaction not itself required to be effected by a registered document, that is, a transaction creating, etc. any right, title or interest in immoveable property of the value of one hundred rupees and upwards.
5. If a document is inadmissible in evidence for want of registration, none of its terms can be admitted in evidence and that to use a document for the purpose of proving an important clause would not be using it as a collateral purpose.
22. The finding of possession is a finding of facts which cannot be interfered while exercising powers under section 100 of Code of Civil Procedure. The defendants/ appellants could not prove their possession as well as the execution of the Rahanama paper no. 18C, therefore, no rights accrued to the appellants/defendants and both the courts below have rightly recorded concurrent findings of possession as well as ownership of the plaintiff/ respondent. Thus, no interference is required.
23. The appeal lacks merit, and is dismissed.
24. No order as to costs.

(Lok Pal Singh, J.) Parul 02.08.2017
